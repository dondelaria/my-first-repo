Hey Mom…

Recipes and stories from 28 years of feeding the people I love.

recipes & stories by Missy

A note before we cook

My name is Missy Delaria, and I learned to cook on the banks of the Niagara River. I grew up in Grand Island, a suburb just outside Buffalo, New York, in a house that looked across the water to Canada. We were a typical family of four — my mother stayed home, my father worked, and my sister was six and a half years older than me, off doing her own things. Truthfully, my mom and grandmother were not big cooks. I treasure the couple of their recipes I still make today, but the kitchen was mostly mine to figure out. I really learned to cook from three places: from Suzie — Mrs. Wiles, a housekeeper in our neighborhood — who gave me my foundation and the real how-to of it; a little bit from my mom; and a great deal from myself, by experimenting, trying things, and simply having the drive and the desire to make good food for the people I love. I started cooking avidly in my early twenties — I can still picture myself baking in my little apartment, turning out sugar cookies and fudge brownies to give away as gifts, made with love and joy, and finding my footing with a few good pasta and pork dishes. It all began with love and curiosity, and it has never stopped. I've never boxed my cooking into one tradition. My roots run through Polish, German, English, Scottish, and American kitchens, with a little Southern worked in too — but honestly, nothing is strictly defined. My cooking is guided by taste and by the combinations of flavors that sing together, never by one specific tradition or recipe. It's a philosophy built on flavor and on the love I put into food to give to other people. And the people who shaped me along the way were many: my grandmother and my mother, Suzie — Mrs. Wiles — and countless friends and family members who each infused something into the way I cook. How much the people at my table enjoy it has always been the only measure that matters.

Don and I have been together for thirty-seven years now. We met as freshmen in 1989 at Babson College. There were very few girls in my class, so they housed us off campus and shuttled us over to the library each morning to begin our classes. One day, across the courtyard outside that library, I spotted a tall, blond boy perched on the wall in Vuarnet sunglasses, pink madras shorts, and a pink polo button-down — and I thought, that guy is really cute. As luck would have it, we ended up in the same class, and before long we were dating. I was the bold one: I asked him on our very first date — to feed the ducks at the duck pond in Wellesley, Massachusetts, and then to a little place that made the best french fries I've ever had, hot and crispy on the outside, tender within, and perfectly salted. A duck-feeding, french-fry first date — that was the beginning of it all. We visited each other's families, were voted best couple in our college, and he proposed on Stockton Island, on Lake Superior. Fittingly, I was down in the galley cooking when he called up to me — 'come up, come up, quick!' He got down on one knee as the sun was setting, and as if it were written in the radio waves, 'Going to the Chapel' came crackling over the ship-to-shore radio at that very moment. It was meant to be.

We married in 1995 in Wayzata, Minnesota — a celebration our friends still say is the one they measure every other wedding against — bought a wonderful little house in Deep Haven, and began building a life of our own. For our honeymoon, we spent three weeks in Thailand, where I took a cooking class at the Oriental and fell head over heels for Thai cooking — beef and broccoli, Thai basil, handmade dumplings, and spices that are hot but never linger. That trip left a lasting mark on the way I cook. In August of 1997 we welcomed our first child, Kendall, and in September of 1999 we completed our family with our twins, Stockton and Larkin — and just like that, we were a family of five. When the children arrived, we had a decision to make: would I keep working, or stay home? We chose for me to stay home, and I have been devoted to that choice ever since.

Cooking in our family always started with one simple desire: to have us all together at the table every single night. Even when the children were babies, we ate dinner together. My rule was that I did not cook multiple meals — the family food was the family food, and the kids ate everything. Nothing was made special just for them. They certainly had their favorites, and once they were old enough they served themselves and could take a 'no thank you helping,' because the way I cooked was balanced and nutritious and I wanted them to have every part of what their growing bodies needed. Each child would fix their plate, carry it to their place, and wait until their father and I had ours, so that we were all seated at the same time. No one rushed through dinner. And every night, we went around the table and each shared one good thing and one bad thing from our day. That little ritual taught us something I hold dear: that good things are meant to be celebrated by the people you love, and the hard things can be solved — or simply comforted — by the people you love. We kept that table, and that tradition, all the way through high school, even when the kids were busy with lives of their own. And there was one more rule in our house: the person who cooks does not clean up. Since I did all the prep and the cooking, having everything ready the moment the kids were hungry and my husband, Don, walked in from work, the cleanup belonged to everyone else. We would all clear our places, and then Don and the kids put the food away, loaded the dishwasher, and washed whatever needed washing. After that the kids went off to their homework, and Don and I would catch up about the day, help with assignments, and finally sit down together. It meant that cooking never felt like a thankless job — it was something our whole family shared in.

Feeding a family of five on a single income took real preparation and thought. It took creativity and a budget that worked. Every week I would sit down and plan our meals, then plan the grocery shopping around them, always making sure there were healthy things in the refrigerator for lunches and snacks. We were lucky enough to have little refrigerators that belonged to the kids, stocked with things like string cheese, juice boxes, Go-Gurts, fruit cups, and applesauce. They were allowed to help themselves to one without asking — and after that, they had to come find me. I took being their mom, and their father's wife, very seriously, and all of it — the planning, the prep, the energy — went into the meals my kids still come back to me for.

Feeding real people also means cooking around real tastes, and ours kept me on my toes. Honestly, my husband is the pickiest eater of us all — no condiments, no cream sauces, no cheese — which is a challenge, because those are exactly the things kids love to pile on their food. But I always found a way, and everyone always had something at the table they were happy to eat. Later in life, our youngest became gluten-free for medical reasons, and I set out to remake our favorites without gluten and to teach myself how to bake gluten-free — especially for her, the one who always swore she had a 'dessert pocket' that simply hadn't been filled yet. You'll find that many of the recipes in this book carry those lessons with them.

The truth is, the seed for all of this was planted next door. Growing up, the family beside us was wonderfully large, and I embraced everything about their life — the Sunday meals, the parties, the constant gathering. That was the life I longed for, and it's the life Don and I built. Caring for people through food is simply my love language. Cooking for others has never once felt like a chore or been hard for me; I love dreaming up meals for all different palates, pulling from every recipe I can find and making them my own, with a few tried-and-true dishes I always turn to for company. And company could be anyone — a neighbor, old friends from high school, parents from the kids' schools. Ours was always the house where people gathered: comfortable, welcoming, doors open. If a kid needed a meal, they came to us. If someone was going through a hard time, a meal was on its way to their door. At the holidays, anyone who couldn't travel had a place at our table for Thanksgiving and Christmas. And every Christmas Day we kept a tradition my mom started — a drop-by open house, where friends could stop in after the presents were unwrapped, kids in tow, to talk about their gifts and their families. It was always such a social, joyful, special time.

For twenty-eight years, my work has been raising our children and feeding our family — putting nutritious, wonderful-tasting, fun food on the table every single day. Food became the center of our home: the everyday dinners, the lunches and after-school snacks, the holidays, the parties, and all the friends and family who gathered around our table along the way. This book is everything I learned in those years, from the technique Suzie first taught me to the dishes my own kids grew up on. And that is exactly where this book gets its name. To this day, my grown children will text me out of the blue: 'Hey Mom…' — and then those three little dots — 'can you send me the recipe for…' It's always one of the things they grew up eating. This book is my answer to every one of those messages, gathered at last in one place. More than anything, it's an invitation. I want to welcome you into my years of experience at the stove — and I hope, in some small way, it helps with yours.

Grandma Rosa's Sunday Gravy

🍴 Serves 6 to 8⏱ 4 hours (mostly hands-off)

My great-grandmother Rosa came over with this in her head, never on paper. "Gravy," in our family, never meant the brown stuff — it meant a pot of tomato sauce simmering with meat from before church until after. The whole house smelled like Sunday. The rule she gave my mother, who gave it to me, was this: never rush the gravy, and always taste it with bread you've torn, not cut. I have no idea why the bread has to be torn. I do it anyway.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 lb pork spare ribs or a pork shoulder chunk
  • 6 Italian sausages
  • 1 onion, finely chopped
  • 4 cloves garlic, minced
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 2 (28 oz) cans whole peeled tomatoes, crushed by hand
  • 1 cup water (swirl it in the cans to get every bit)
  • 2 teaspoons salt, plus more to taste
  • A small handful of fresh basil
  • A pinch of sugar, only if the tomatoes are sharp

Steps

  1. Heat the oil in a heavy pot. Brown the pork and sausage on all sides, then set them aside. Don't crowd the pot.
  2. Lower the heat. Cook the onion in the drippings until soft and golden, about 8 minutes, then add the garlic for one more minute.
  3. Stir in the tomato paste and let it darken for a minute or two — this is where the deep flavor hides.
  4. Add the crushed tomatoes, the swirl water, and the salt. Return the meat to the pot.
  5. Bring to a bare bubble, then drop to the lowest simmer. Partly cover and cook 3 to 4 hours, stirring now and then so the bottom doesn't catch.
  6. Pull the meat out, tear the basil in, taste, and adjust salt (and that pinch of sugar if needed). Serve the sauce over pasta and the meat on a platter alongside.

✨ Make It Your Own

  • No pork? Use beef short ribs, meatballs, or skip the meat entirely and add mushrooms for body.
  • Want it weeknight-fast? Brown just the sausage, skip the long braise, and simmer 45 minutes.
  • We like it smooth; my sister likes it chunky. Blend half, or none, to your taste.
  • A parmesan rind dropped in while it simmers adds a savory depth that's hard to name and easy to love.

Notes

This freezes beautifully. I make a double batch and freeze flat in bags so a Sunday dinner is never more than a reheat away.

Sheet-Pan Lemon Chicken & Potatoes

🍴 Serves 4⏱ 45 minutesGF Gluten-Free

This is the recipe I reach for on the nights when "Hey Mom, what's for dinner?" arrives and I have absolutely nothing planned. One pan, no fancy steps, and the oven does the work while I referee homework. It has saved more weeknights than I can count, and it tastes like I tried much harder than I did.

Ingredients

  • 6 bone-in, skin-on chicken thighs
  • 1.5 lbs baby potatoes, halved
  • 1 lemon (half juiced, half sliced into thin rounds)
  • 3 tablespoons olive oil
  • 4 cloves garlic, smashed
  • 1 teaspoon dried oregano
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • Plenty of black pepper
  • A handful of fresh parsley, chopped, to finish

Steps

  1. Heat the oven to 425°F (220°C).
  2. On a large sheet pan, toss the potatoes with 2 tablespoons oil, half the garlic, salt, and pepper. Push them to the edges.
  3. Rub the chicken with the remaining oil, oregano, lemon juice, salt, and pepper. Nestle it skin-side up in the middle, tuck the lemon slices and remaining garlic around it.
  4. Roast 35 to 40 minutes, until the chicken reads 175°F and the skin is crisp and the potatoes are tender.
  5. Scatter parsley over the top and bring the whole pan to the table.

✨ Make It Your Own

  • Swap the potatoes for any sturdy vegetable — carrots, fennel, green beans (add green beans in the last 15 minutes so they don't shrivel).
  • Boneless thighs work too; just check them at 25 minutes.
  • Make it Greek with a handful of olives and a crumble of feta at the end, or Moroccan with a teaspoon of cumin and a pinch of cinnamon.
  • This is already gluten-free — serve it with crusty bread only if your table can have it.

Notes

The lemon slices get a little crispy and caramelized in the oven. The kids fight over them. Make extra.

Everyday GF Banana Bread (A Lesson in Swaps)

🍴 Serves 8 to 10⏱ 1 hour 15 minutesGF Gluten-Free

The flourless cake taught us to start with what's naturally gluten-free. But sometimes you genuinely need to swap — and this banana bread is my teaching loaf for exactly that. The secret isn't one magic flour; it's understanding what gluten did in the original recipe (it gave structure and a little chew) and replacing that job, not just the flour. A good 1:1 GF blend that already contains xanthan gum does most of the work. The rest is a few small habits. Once you learn them here, you can convert almost any quick bread, muffin, or pancake in your old recipe box.

Ingredients

  • 3 very ripe bananas, mashed (the browner, the better)
  • 1/3 cup melted butter or neutral oil
  • 3/4 cup brown sugar
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla
  • 1 3/4 cups gluten-free 1:1 baking flour (one that includes xanthan gum)
  • 1 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon cinnamon

Steps

  1. Heat the oven to 350°F (175°C) and line a loaf pan with parchment.
  2. Mash the bananas in a big bowl, then stir in the melted butter, brown sugar, eggs, and vanilla.
  3. Add the GF flour, baking soda, salt, and cinnamon. Stir just until no dry streaks remain — GF batters don't get tough, but overmixing still makes them gummy.
  4. The key teaching: let the batter rest 10 to 15 minutes before baking. This lets the GF starches hydrate, which is the difference between gritty and tender.
  5. Pour into the pan and bake 55 to 65 minutes, until a toothpick comes out clean. GF loaves often need a few extra minutes — trust the toothpick, not the clock.
  6. Cool in the pan 15 minutes, then lift out and cool fully before slicing (slicing warm GF bread makes it crumble).

✨ Make It Your Own

  • The swap lesson, generalized: to convert one of your own quick breads, replace the wheat flour 1:1 with a GF blend, add a 10-minute batter rest, and expect a slightly longer bake. That's 90% of it.
  • Fold in chocolate chips, walnuts, or blueberries.
  • No GF blend on hand? A mix of 1 cup oat flour + 3/4 cup almond flour works, though the crumb is denser.
  • Make it dairy-free with oil, and confirm your oats/flours are certified GF if needed.

Notes

Slices freeze perfectly. I wrap them individually so a GF breakfast is one toaster-cycle away on a busy morning.

Flourless Chocolate Cake (The One Nobody Believes Is GF)

🍴 Serves 10⏱ 1 hour, plus coolingGF Gluten-Free

When my niece was diagnosed with celiac, the thing she cried about wasn't bread — it was that she thought she'd never have a "real" birthday cake again. So I went looking, and I learned the most freeing lesson in gluten-free baking: the best GF desserts often aren't substitutes at all. This cake never had flour to begin with. She blew out the candles, took one bite, and said "this is better." It is. This is the first of my gluten-free teachings: start with what's naturally gluten-free before you reach for a swap.

Ingredients

  • 8 oz good dark chocolate (70%), chopped
  • 1 cup (2 sticks) unsalted butter
  • 1 1/4 cups granulated sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon vanilla extract
  • 6 large eggs
  • 1 cup unsweetened cocoa powder, plus more for dusting

Steps

  1. Heat the oven to 350°F (175°C). Butter a 9-inch springform pan and line the bottom with parchment.
  2. Melt the chocolate and butter together gently — a double boiler or short microwave bursts — and stir smooth.
  3. Whisk in the sugar, salt, and vanilla. Let it cool for a couple of minutes so it doesn't cook the eggs.
  4. Whisk in the eggs one at a time until the batter is glossy, then sift in the cocoa and fold just until combined.
  5. Pour into the pan and bake 35 to 40 minutes, until the top is set with a thin crust and the center is just barely firm.
  6. Cool completely in the pan, then dust with cocoa. It's even better the next day.

✨ Make It Your Own

  • A teaspoon of espresso powder deepens the chocolate without tasting like coffee.
  • Add the grated zest of an orange, or a pinch of cinnamon and cayenne for a Mexican-chocolate version.
  • Serve with whipped cream and raspberries, or a scoop of ice cream for the kids.
  • Naturally gluten-free already — just confirm your cocoa and chocolate are certified GF if cross-contamination is a concern.

Notes

A clean knife wiped between slices gives those bakery-perfect wedges. Warm the knife under hot water for the cleanest cut.

Baked Brie with Honey & Toasted Walnuts

🍴 Serves 10 to 12 (as an appetizer)⏱ 25 minutes

I brought this to a neighborhood potluck the first year we moved in, mostly because it was the only fancy-looking thing I knew how to make. It vanished in about nine minutes, and three people asked for the recipe before I had my coat off. It has been my "introduce myself to new people" dish ever since. It looks like effort; it is barely any.

Ingredients

  • 1 wheel of brie (8 to 12 oz)
  • 1/3 cup honey
  • 1/2 cup walnuts, roughly chopped
  • 2 tablespoons dried cranberries
  • A sprig of fresh rosemary, leaves chopped
  • Crackers, sliced baguette, and apple slices, to serve

Steps

  1. Heat the oven to 350°F (175°C). Place the brie on a parchment-lined baking dish (or a small cast-iron skillet for drama).
  2. Bake 12 to 15 minutes, until the center gives way when you press it gently — soft but not collapsed.
  3. While it bakes, toast the walnuts in a dry pan until fragrant, about 4 minutes.
  4. Move the warm brie to a serving board. Drizzle with honey, then pile on the walnuts, cranberries, and rosemary.
  5. Serve immediately with crackers, bread, and apple slices for dipping and scooping.

✨ Make It Your Own

  • For a gluten-free spread, serve with seed crackers, apple and pear slices, and cucumber rounds instead of bread.
  • Swap the topping with the seasons: figs and balsamic in late summer, hot pepper jelly for a kick, or brown sugar and pecans for a dessert-y version.
  • Wrap the whole wheel in puff pastry before baking if you want the showstopper version (not gluten-free).

Notes

You can assemble the toppings ahead and bake the brie right as guests arrive. Warm cheese waits for no one — serve it the moment it hits the board.
